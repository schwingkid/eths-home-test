Knowledge becomes responsibility when another person is waiting.

Youth Technology Corps teaches high school students a technical skill and then asks them to hand it to someone else — a younger student, a family, a senior center, a classroom on another continent. Twenty‑eight years, one idea, passed down.

The handoff is the education.

Most technology programs measure what a student can do. We measure what a student can pass on. A member arrives knowing nothing about the inside of a computer. Within a year, they are the one explaining it — to a freshman, to a family picking up a refurbished machine, to a class in Namibia over video.

That inversion is the whole method. Students don't really own a skill until they've given it away, and a program built on that principle compounds instead of graduating. Every year's members become the next year's teachers.

It also solves the problem every access program runs into: cost. The teaching is free because the teachers are the students.

01

Learn it

Members are taught to diagnose, repair and rebuild donated computers, and to work with robotics and microcontroller kits.

02

Teach it

Each member teaches what they learned — to newer members, to Evanston families and seniors, and to partner classrooms abroad.

03

Lead it

Experienced members run sessions, train new teachers and take responsibility for a site or a class of their own.

04

Repeat

Students they taught become teachers themselves. The Global Learning Alliance is what happens when that keeps going across borders.

Where it reaches

One method, nine countries.

The Global Learning Alliance is the network that forms when students who were taught begin teaching elsewhere. It is not a franchise. It is the same handoff, repeated across borders.

  • Evanston, ILThe flagship chapter at ETHS. Schools, senior centers, churches and community organizations since 2009.
  • NamibiaYears of refurbished machines, then ETHS students teaching in the classrooms that received them.
  • NigeriaLagos opened in 2026 with fifteen students at Victokev Schools, taught by a student trained by ETHS members.
  • Ghana · EthiopiaPartner classrooms and student-teachers inside the Alliance.
  • Mozambique · MexicoKit donations and exchange work that seeded further classrooms.
  • El Salvador · New OrleansDirect donations of student-refurbished machines to communities in need.

The flagship

Evanston Township High School.

ETHS is where the model runs at full strength: a student-run club, now in its seventeenth year without a gap, operating out of clubroom H120A. Members refurbish, teach, lead sessions in the community, and travel.

It is being built as the first Flagship Global Learning Center — a complete, documented version of the program that another school or university chapter can adopt rather than invent.

College Partner Program

Universities teaching into high school clubrooms.

Georgia Tech is the first.

Beginning this school year, Student Makers at Georgia Tech will teach STEM sessions virtually into the ETHS clubroom, with a faculty advisor serving on our grant advisory committee.

The point is not one partnership. It is a shape that repeats: a university makerspace chapter adopts a high school YTC club, a faculty member attaches, and undergraduates get a real teaching practicum while high school students get people only a few years ahead of them. We intend to make that a one-page offer any university can say yes to.

Where it comes from. Youth Technology Corps was founded in 1998 by David Finkel, who has spent twenty-eight years proving that the fastest way to make a skill stick is to require a student to hand it to somebody else. The Global Learning Alliance carried that method across borders. ETHS is where it now runs at full strength.
